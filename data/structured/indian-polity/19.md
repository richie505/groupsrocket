=== TOPIC indian-polity:19 | Judicial review, doctrines and landmark cases

## Judicial review | from: 51, 67
# 1. Meaning
- **Inherent constitutional power** of the **higher judiciary** to examine the constitutionality of **legislative enactments** and **executive orders** (actions) — and declare them **void** when they contravene the Constitution
- **Vested jointly** in the **Supreme Court (Article 32)** and the **High Courts (Article 226)** — **not** in the Supreme Court alone
- **Origin:** **USA** — **Marbury v. Madison (1803)**, Chief Justice **John Marshall**
- **India:** **Constitutional supremacy** with judicial review of **primary legislation**
  - **UK:** **Parliamentary sovereignty** — no traditional judicial review; primary statutes **cannot be struck down**
- **Associated Articles:** **13, 32, 136, 137, 226 and 227**
# 2. Textual basis
- **Article 13(2):** The **primary textual foundation** — laws abridging Fundamental Rights are **void pro tanto**; an express basis for striking down **ultra vires** laws
- **Article 32:** SC writ jurisdiction for Fundamental Rights — a **constitutional remedy** and part of the **Basic Structure**
- **Article 226:** HC writ jurisdiction — Fundamental Rights **and any other legal right**; **wider** than Article 32
- **Article 137:** SC **reviews its own** judgments or orders
- **Article 142:** **Complete Justice** decrees — **inherent equitable jurisdiction**, **distinct** from judicial review
# 3. Basic Structure status
- **Kesavananda Bharati (1973)**, **Minerva Mills (1980)** and **L. Chandra Kumar (1997):** Judicial review under **Articles 32 and 226** is an **integral, inviolable** part of the Basic Structure
- Parliament **cannot abrogate** it even through **Article 368**; **constitutional amendments** are themselves subject to judicial review on the **Basic Structure** touchstone
# 4. Ninth Schedule
- **First Amendment Act (1951):** Added **Articles 31A and 31B** and the **Ninth Schedule** — to insulate **agrarian** and **land-reform** laws from judicial review on Fundamental Rights grounds
- Enacted largely to overcome early judicial interpretations restricting **state welfare** and **land acquisition** measures
# 5. What judicial review is not
- **Not** questioning the **political wisdom**, **policy merits** or **advisability** of legislation
- **Not** automatic **pre-assent** scrutiny of Bills; **not** an ordinary **appellate** proceeding
- **Federal role:** Reinforces the **federal principle**, preserves the **distribution of powers**, enforces **constitutional limits** — it is **not** a **unitary** element
  - **Unitary elements:** **All India Services**, the **Governor's appointment**, **Emergency provisions**

## The amendment battle and the Basic Structure | from: 24, 41, 67
# 1. Can Parliament amend Fundamental Rights?
| Case | Year | Held |
|---|---|---|
| **Shankari Prasad v. Union of India** | **1951** | **Yes** — upheld Parliament's power under **Article 368** and the **First Amendment**; "law" in **Article 13(2)** does **not** include constitutional amendments (Article 13 vs Article 368) |
| **Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan** | **1965** | **Yes** — reaffirmed the power to amend **any part** of the Constitution; upheld the First Amendment (**Article 368**) |
| **Golaknath v. State of Punjab** | **1967** | **No** — **reversed (overruled)** the earlier cases; a constitutional amendment **is law** under **Article 13(2)**; Parliament **cannot abridge or take away** any Fundamental Right — the **first** case to limit the amending power |
| **Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala** | **1973** | **Yes, but** — Parliament can amend **any part**, including Fundamental Rights, but **cannot alter, damage or destroy** the **Basic Structure** |
| **Minerva Mills v. Union of India** | **1980** | **Reaffirmed** the Basic Structure — a **limited amending power** is itself a **basic feature** |
# 2. Kesavananda Bharati (24 April 1973)
- **Largest bench:** **13 judges**; decided by a **7:6** majority
- **Propounded** the **Basic Structure Doctrine** — linked to **Article 368 (Part XX)** and the **24th Amendment**
- Recognised the **Preamble** as part of the Constitution
- Parliament's **constituent power** is **plenary but limited** — it cannot abrogate the **essential foundation** or **basic features**
# 3. Minerva Mills (1980)
- Struck down clauses of the **42nd Amendment** — **clauses (4) and (5)** of **Article 368**, and clauses giving **absolute precedence** to **DPSP** over Fundamental Rights
- **Restored the balance** between **Part III** and **Part IV**
# 4. Traps
- ⚠ "Basic Structure is explicitly defined in **Part XX**" — **false**: it was **judicially formulated**
- ⚠ "Fundamental Rights can **never** be amended after Kesavananda" — **false**: they can be, if the amendment does not violate the Basic Structure

## Constitutional doctrines | from: 67
- **Nature:** Most doctrines are **judicially evolved** — **not** expressly defined or written in the Constitution
| Doctrine | Origin | Articles | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Basic Structure** | **Kesavananda Bharati (1973)** | **Article 368** (Part XX) | Parliament cannot destroy the **basic features** |
| **Judicial Review** | **Marbury v. Madison (1803)** — **John Marshall** (USA) | **13, 32, 136, 137, 226, 227** | The **higher judiciary** (SC and HCs) adjudicates the validity of **legislative acts** and **executive actions** |
| **Severability** | **A.K. Gopalan (1950)** and **State of Bombay v. F.N. Balsara (1951)** | **13(1) and 13(2)** | Only the **offending portion** of a statute is void; the **valid portion** survives if it can be separated |
| **Eclipse** | **Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v. State of M.P. (1955)** | **13(1)** | **Pre-constitutional** laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights are **not dead ab initio** — they stay **overshadowed (dormant)** and revive if the Fundamental Right is **amended** |
| **Pith and Substance** | **Prafulla Kumar v. Bank of Commerce (1947)** and **State of Rajasthan v. G. Chawla (1959)** | **Article 246** and the **Seventh Schedule** | Resolves **overlaps** between legislative fields by the law's **true nature and essence**; **incidental encroachment** on another List does not decide validity |
| **Colorable Legislation** | **Kameshwar Singh v. State of Bihar (1952)** | **Article 246** | "What **cannot be done directly** cannot be done **indirectly**" — transgression of legislative competence under a **permissible guise** |

## Rule of Law and constitutional government | from: 75
# 1. Origin
- **A.V. Dicey** (British jurist) — **Introduction to the Study of the Law of the Constitution** (**1885**)
- **Borrowed from Britain** with: **Parliamentary Government**, **Single Citizenship**, **Cabinet System**, **Prerogative Writs**
- **In India:** Anchored mainly in **Article 14** — **equality before law** and **equal protection of the laws**, for **citizens and foreigners**
- **Basic Structure:** Affirmed in **Kesavananda Bharati (1973)** and **Indira Nehru Gandhi (1975)**
# 2. Dicey's three elements
- **1. Absence of arbitrary power** (limitation of powers): **Supremacy of regular law** over arbitrary power — no punishment except for a distinct **breach of law** proved in the **ordinary legal manner**
  - ⚠ "The State has inherent unlimited police powers" — **false**
- **2. Equality before the law:** All classes **equally subject** to the **ordinary law**, administered by **ordinary courts** — **no one above the law**
  - Dicey **opposed** the French **Droit Administratif**; ⚠ "executive officials are subject only to special administrative tribunals" — **false**
- **3. Primacy of individual rights** (liberty and civil rights): The Constitution is the **result** of individual rights defined and enforced by **courts** — rights come from **judicial decisions**, not from the Constitution
  - **UK:** Constitution results from judicial decisions; **India:** the Constitution is the **source** of rights — India **reverses** the third premise and adopts the first two intact
- ⚠ "**People's responsibility to the Government**" is **not** a pillar — delete it from the framework; the Rule of Law **restrains** the government and does not expand citizens' obedience
# 3. Rule of Law vs Rule by Law
| Rule of Law | Rule by Law |
|---|---|
| **Government under law** — the government itself is **subordinate** to law | **Government above law** — law as an **instrument (weapon)** of state control; the ruler stays **above** the law |
| Laws must be **just, fair and non-arbitrary** | Governs citizens only |
# 4. Equality before law vs equal protection of laws
| Equality before law | Equal protection of the laws |
|---|---|
| **British** origin | **American** origin |
| **Negative** concept — **no special privilege** for anyone | **Positive** concept — **equal treatment** in equal circumstances; permits **reasonable classification** |
# 5. Procedure and discretion
- **"Procedure established by law"** (text of **Article 21**): A law is valid if made by the correct **legislative procedure** — **A.K. Gopalan (1950)**
- **Due process** after **Maneka Gandhi (1978):** Law must be **just, fair and reasonable** and free from **procedural arbitrariness**
- **Unguided, uncontrolled or uncanalised discretion** given to an executive authority violates **non-arbitrariness** under **Article 14** and subverts the Rule of Law — discretion without **objective standards** or **judicial safeguards** is **ultra vires** Article 14
# 6. Constitutional government and PIL
- **Constitutional Government = Limited Government:** **Defines, restrains and checks** power — provisions prevent **executive tyranny**
- **Public Interest Litigation:** Promotes and strengthens **access to justice** for disadvantaged persons — but is **not indispensable** for the Rule of Law, which existed before PIL through **Articles 14, 32 and 226**
